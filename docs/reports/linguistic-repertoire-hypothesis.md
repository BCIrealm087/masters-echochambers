# Can cultural references help explain who amplifies whom?

**Prospective thesis extension · 2026-11-26**

While examining the earlier hashtag co-occurrence graphs, the researcher noticed tightly connected sets of tags that appeared to draw on different cultural vocabularies. Examples recalled from that work include football metaphors, references to Marvel films and expressions associated with different strands of Brazilian political discourse. These are exploratory observations from visual inspection. Their prevalence, interpretation and relationship to account communities remain to be measured. [Prior study](../prior-study.md)

The thesis hypothesis is that **accounts can share ways of expressing a political position, and those ways of speaking may help explain whose material they amplify later**. A *linguistic repertoire* here means a recurring combination of references, expressions and rhetorical moves in observed communication. Several repertoires can coexist in one account, and different groups can draw on the same repertoire. The observed use of a reference depends on its speaker and context. [Eckert on the social meaning of variation](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9841.2008.00374.x)

## Three tests with distinct outcomes

| Test | Evidence to learn from | Evidence to evaluate on | A result that would narrow the claim |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Cultural coherence.** Do accounts in independently discovered repost groups use distinguishable repertoires? | Freeze repost-only groups. Develop the features and annotation guide on a discovery sample of authored Portuguese posts. | Estimate repertoire prevalence and between-group differences in later independently coded posts. Compare within the same candidate, stance, topic and event where groups have common coverage. Check whether the pattern is distributed across authors and survives deduplication. | The difference is concentrated in a copied slogan, a debate or a few authors, or later uses are too ambiguous to validate the category. |
| **Structural recovery or extension.** Do past hashtags and authored language reveal reproducible organization? | Build an account–hashtag or account–feature graph from discovery material only. Compare its groups with the independent interaction partition on their common accounts. | Test language-defined or combined groups on later reposts. Evaluate tag-defined groups on later original body text with hashtags stripped. Assess overlap, splits, cross-group use and assignment coverage. | Groups agree only when both views include identical slogans or reposted material. Stable language groups that differ from repost groups can still identify a discourse pattern that cuts across sharing boundaries. |
| **Incremental prediction.** Does repertoire similarity improve held-out source choice? | Learn an account profile from pre-cutoff authored posts. Use the same history, context and popularity weights as the repost predictor. | Compare paired log scores on the same later reposts and candidate authors. Include unseen sharer–author pairs with usable past profiles, and report coverage separately. | Generic topic and stance features explain the gain, or gains vanish when repeated originals or sources are removed. |

For the first test, report both unadjusted group profiles and comparisons within comparable topics, days and stances. Group differences may be mediated by what people talk about. The matched comparison asks the narrower question of how people express a similar position about a similar event. If comparable examples are sparse, report the resulting limit on the comparison. [Discourse design](reflexive-report-2.md) · [Altakrori et al. on separating topic and style in evaluation](https://aclanthology.org/2021.findings-emnlp.359/)

A primary outcome could be the percentage-point difference in the prevalence of a particular reference–function pair between comparable groups, averaging over authors. For a football metaphor used to mock a candidate, first score each participating author's fraction of comparable posts with that code. Average across authors and keep prolific speakers from dominating the contrast. Report uncertainty using authors as the resampling unit for a fixed window, plus a sensitivity excluding near-duplicate slogans and major sources. Confirmatory use across several codes and groups calls for a short list defined in advance and a multiple-comparison plan. A recurrent combination of features can also be assessed through prediction of frozen group membership from later writing, evaluating author-disjoint groups of examples to distinguish a shared repertoire from identification of individual writers.

## From a qualitative reading to a measurable feature

1. **Keep the speaker and communication channel explicit.** Separate hashtags, authored body text, text quoted from another speaker, and material amplified by repost. Keep an attribution field for quotes and replies. The same slogan appearing in a campaign message, a hostile quotation and a news report can have three different functions.
2. **Code the reference and its function separately.** For each relevant post–target pair, retain candidate target, expressed stance, topic, reference domain, rhetorical use and an evidence span. Possible reference domains begin with football, film or pop culture, and historically situated political vocabularies. Allow multiple codes, emergent categories, no reference and uncertain cases. A football term in a match report and that same term used to ridicule a debate answer deserve different codes. Political vocabulary requires examples in context before interpreting it as associated with a particular tradition.
3. **Build an independent reference on a bounded sample.** Develop the codebook on discovery examples. Include explicit, ambiguous and counterexamples. Ask Brazilian Portuguese readers familiar with the election to annotate a shared subset independently and blind to community labels. Use a stratified probability sample for prevalence and add a separate challenge sample for rare and ambiguous uses. Record agreement per code and adjudicate only after preserving the original judgments. Treat unreliable categories as qualitative evidence.
4. **Start with interpretable features.** Compare normalized hashtag frequencies, character or word n-grams, and a small set of validated reference and function indicators. Treat text embeddings as a second representation and evaluate them against these features. Keep raw and masked variants with hashtags, candidate names, handles and recurring query terms removed as appropriate. Fit vocabularies, frequency weights and feature extractors before evaluation. If automated annotation is used, report tested precision and recall by code and group, abstention coverage and errors under sarcasm or quotation.
5. **Count people as well as messages.** Deduplicate source IDs and near-identical slogan or meme templates for the independence checks. Preserve counts for a separate amplification analysis. Report the average account’s repertoire, distinct originals and repost-weighted prevalence. A pattern concentrated in one circulated item has a different interpretation from a pattern found in independent posts by many accounts. Coverage by group, date, role and channel comes first. The host has text and media metadata. Historical media availability and attributable text coverage still need auditing. [Host inventory](m2-repost-coverage-audit.md#host-inventory-completed-2026-11-26)

## Where language enters the graph

Keep the original hashtag–hashtag graph as a map of co-occurring expressions. To study accounts, begin with a bipartite graph whose two node types are accounts and linguistic features. One edge records an account's use of a feature in its own writing. Weight by distinct uses with account-level normalization and test down-weighting ubiquitous tags. A second view can represent features in distinct originals an account amplifies. The two views distinguish production from circulation.

A first account-to-account comparison can use similarity between the authored profiles when both accounts have enough text. Use sparse nearest-neighbor similarities if the full pairwise graph is too large. A similarity edge means two accounts use similar expressions. A directed repost edge records an action. Compare the two graphs before attempting an attributed or multilayer model. A joint model must use discovery data only, and its added value can be measured on the same later actions as the repost-only and language-only models. An account with too little writing remains uncovered by this evidence. Evaluate this coverage explicitly. [Graph methods](reflexive-report-6.md)

## Where language enters prediction

Let the registered history model provide probabilities over candidate authors for a particular sharer. Add a learned coefficient for common repost-group membership and a separate coefficient for similarity between the two accounts' **earlier** authored profiles, then renormalize over the same candidate set. Start with the original predictor and compare versions with topic and stance, explicit repertoire features, and both repost membership and repertoire features. Make the primary incremental comparison **history + repost group + topic/stance** against that same model **plus repertoire features**. Refit feature construction, group assignments and model parameters inside chronological development folds and freeze them before final evaluation.

Report mean paired log-score gain, uncertainty clustered by sharer and sensitivity to highly reused sources. Use the same scored events and candidate sets within each comparison. Include a breakdown by dyad seen in discovery, and compare first on accounts with enough pre-cutoff writing to construct profiles. A new relationship between two known accounts is a different case from a new account with no earlier language. If group-level results drive a claim, report which groups and which features account for it.

For the current author prediction task, both profiles must come from material available before the cutoff. The text or image of the item actually chosen in the holdout can describe the observed sharing event. A separate item-choice task could incorporate content for **every** eligible candidate post already published when the action occurred, with its availability rule defined explicitly. Historical archive membership by itself supplies a candidate set for analysis and carries no evidence about what the sharer saw. [Prediction protocol](m3-repost-pilot-specification.md#prediction-beyond-prior-ties)

## First experiment and interpretation

Begin with hashtags and attributable authored text from a small set of frozen repost groups that have overlapping topics and sufficient accounts. Use ordinary examples alongside the most salient clusters. Freeze two or three reliably coded repertoire families and retain a comparison group with weak or mixed signals. Validate their prevalence across distinct authors on later posts, then evaluate one pre-period repertoire predictor. Use a fresh period for confirmation if the original holdout has already informed feature or model choices.

If images add material omitted by text, audit recoverability and then test OCR of meme captions or recurring visual templates in a small paired sample. Check whether the visual view adds evidence among the same posts after controlling for duplicate templates and text. The first experiment can establish a language contribution using hashtags and body text.

A recurring repertoire across many accounts would establish a visible pattern in communication. Gain beyond past ties and topic or stance would show that the pattern carries additional information about observed amplification. Repertoires can also cross political and network boundaries. A stronger echo-chamber interpretation would require independent political evidence and credible evidence about comparable opposing material people had an opportunity to share. The M3 pilot supplies the interaction baseline for these later tests. [Conceptual criteria](reflexive-report-8.md)
